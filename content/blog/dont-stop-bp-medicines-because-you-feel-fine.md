"Ab toh BP normal hai. Do din se dawai nahi li, phir bhi normal hai. Dekha?" Papa says it with quiet pride, like he's won something. He points at the BP machine on the side table as proof. And you're not sure what to say, because he does look fine.

This is one of the most common things families hear about BP medicines. It sounds logical. It's also a trap.

## Why high BP feels like nothing

High blood pressure is often called a silent condition because most people don't feel it. There's no pain, no fever, no obvious sign. You can have raised BP for years and feel completely normal.

That's exactly what makes it tricky. With a headache, you take a tablet, the pain goes, you stop. BP doesn't work like that. Feeling fine tells you very little about what your blood pressure is doing.

## "Normal" might be the medicine working

Here's the part that's easy to miss. If Papa's BP reading is normal while he's on medicine, that's usually because the medicine is doing its job.

It's like a ceiling fan on a hot Lucknow afternoon. The room feels comfortable, so you think, "It's not that hot, let me switch it off." Then the heat comes back. The fan was the reason it felt fine.

For many people, BP medicine is meant to be taken long-term, often written as "continue" on the prescription. It manages the condition. It doesn't usually cure it in a few weeks.

## What stopping on your own can do

Stopping or skipping BP medicines without a doctor's guidance can let blood pressure rise again, sometimes without any warning feeling. Some BP medicines are also not meant to be stopped suddenly at all.

This isn't about scaring anyone. It's just that decisions about BP medicines need someone who can see the full picture: readings over time, other conditions, other medicines.

So the rule is simple: **don't stop, skip or reduce a BP medicine because you feel fine. Talk to the doctor first.**

## If he genuinely wants to come off it

Sometimes the wish to stop has a real reason behind it. Listen for it.

- **"Isse chakkar aata hai."** Side effects like dizziness are worth telling the doctor about. There may be other options.
- **"Itni saari goliyan."** Too many tablets is a fair complaint. Ask the doctor if the routine can be simplified.
- **"Maine weight kam kiya, walk karta hoon."** Lifestyle changes really can help BP. The doctor may review the medicine based on readings. But that's their decision to make with him.
- **"Mehngi padti hai."** Cost is a real issue. Ask the doctor or pharmacist about more affordable options, rather than quietly stopping.

In every case, the answer is the same: take the reason to the doctor, not to the dustbin.

## Help him track, not guess

A home BP machine can be a useful tool, as long as the readings go to the doctor. Some simple habits:

- Measure at roughly the same times of day, sitting calmly
- Write down the date, time and reading
- Bring the log to every check-up
- Don't change anything based on a single reading

If a reading is very high and he has symptoms like chest pain, severe headache, sudden weakness, confusion, blurred vision or trouble breathing, that's urgent. Call 112 or go to a hospital immediately.

## The takeaway

Feeling fine on BP medicine often means the medicine is working. Don't stop or skip it without the doctor. If there's a reason he wants to change, take that reason to the clinic and let the doctor decide.
