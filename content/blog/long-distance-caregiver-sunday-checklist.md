Sunday, 11 am. You're on your second chai, the laundry is spinning, and the weekly call home is about to start. It usually goes like this: twenty minutes of who got married, five minutes of the neighbour's dog, and thirty seconds of "aur dawai sab theek chal rahi hai?" before someone has to go.

That thirty seconds is doing a lot of heavy lifting. What if you gave it a proper ten minutes, with a list?

## Why a fixed weekly check works

Daily calls about health get tiring for everyone. Parents start to feel monitored, and you start to dread the call. But if health gets a calm, predictable slot once a week, the daily calls can go back to being about life.

A checklist also means you don't rely on remembering what to ask. You're tired on Sundays too.

## The checklist

Keep it on your phone. Run through it in order. Most weeks it'll take less than ten minutes.

**1. Medicines in hand**
- Is the pill organiser filled for the coming week?
- Count what's left in each strip. Will anything run out in the next 10 days?
- Any new medicine started this week, by any doctor? Get a photo of that prescription.

**2. Doses this week**
- Were any doses missed? Ask gently, without blame.
- Was there any confusion about timing, like before or after food?
- Any new side effects they've noticed? If yes, note it for the doctor rather than deciding anything yourself.

**3. Numbers**
- If there's a BP machine or sugar meter at home, ask for this week's readings.
- Write them down with the date. A simple notes file is enough.
- Don't panic over a single number. Patterns matter more, and the doctor is the right person to read them.

**4. Appointments and tests**
- Any check-up, blood test or follow-up due in the next month?
- Who's taking them? Does that person know?
- Are old reports somewhere easy to find before the visit?

**5. The human bit**
- How's their sleep? Appetite? Mood?
- Have they been out of the house this week?
- Is anything bothering them that they haven't said?

## How to ask without it feeling like an audit

Tone matters more than the list. Start with something light, then move into it naturally. "Chalo, weekly dawai wala round kar lete hain" sounds like a routine, not an inspection.

If a parent is at home with a sibling, spouse or helper, involve them. The person who fills the pill box usually knows more than the person who takes the pills.

And thank them. Seriously. "Thank you for sending the reading, Papa" goes a long way in getting the next one.

## What to do with what you learn

The point of the checklist isn't to collect information. It's to act on the small things before they become big ones.

- Strip running low? Message the chemist or the sibling nearby today, not Thursday.
- New prescription from a different doctor? Make sure every doctor knows about all the medicines, and ask the pharmacist to check for anything that shouldn't be taken together.
- Readings looking different from usual? Share the log with the doctor and let them decide.

For anything that sounds urgent, like chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing or fainting, don't wait for next Sunday. Tell them to call 112 or get to a hospital immediately.

## Keep a running note

After each call, add two or three lines to a single running note: date, any changes, anything to follow up. Over a few months this becomes surprisingly useful. When a doctor asks, "Since when has this been happening?", you'll actually have an answer.

## The takeaway

Ten focused minutes on a Sunday can replace a week of anxious guessing. Keep the list short, the tone warm, and write things down.
