You sign up for a new health app for Mummy. A screen full of tiny text appears. You scroll to the bottom and tap "I agree" without reading a word. Later you wonder: where does her prescription actually go? Who can see her sugar readings? Can you get it deleted if you change your mind?

India now has a law that tries to answer exactly these questions. It's called the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, usually shortened to the DPDP Act, and it was passed in 2023. Here's what it means for your family, in plain language. This is a general overview, not legal advice.

## What the law is about

The DPDP Act sets rules for how organisations collect and use digital personal data about people in India. That includes things like your name, phone number and, importantly, health details stored in apps and systems.

It uses two terms worth knowing:

- **Data principal:** the person the data is about. That's you, or your parent
- **Data fiduciary:** the company or organisation deciding how the data is used, like an app, a lab or a hospital system

The basic idea is simple. Your data belongs to your life, and anyone handling it has to follow rules and stay accountable.

## Consent has to mean something

Under the Act, organisations generally need your consent to process your personal data, and that consent is supposed to be free, specific, informed and clear. In practice, that means:

- You should be told what data is being collected and why
- The notice should be understandable, and available in English or Indian languages listed in the Constitution
- You should be able to withdraw consent later, and withdrawing should be about as easy as giving it

So the "I agree" screen is supposed to actually tell you something. It's worth reading the short version, at least.

## Purpose limitation

Data collected for one purpose shouldn't be used for something unrelated. If you share Papa's prescription so an app can set dose reminders, that's the purpose. Organisations are also expected to delete data once it's no longer needed for that purpose, unless another law requires them to keep it.

## Your rights over your data

The Act gives individuals a set of rights. In general terms, you can:

- **Access** a summary of your personal data and how it's being processed
- **Correct and update** data that is wrong or incomplete
- **Erase** data that is no longer needed, or when you withdraw consent
- **Get grievances addressed** by the organisation, which should have a way to raise complaints
- **Nominate** someone to exercise your rights if you die or become unable to

If the organisation doesn't resolve your complaint, there is a Data Protection Board of India that handles such matters.

## What this means for families

A lot of family health care involves one person managing data for another. A few practical things to keep in mind:

- When you set up an app for a parent, explain it to them and make sure they're comfortable. It's their data
- The Act has stricter rules for children's data, including consent from a parent or guardian
- Keep track of which apps and services have your family's health information
- If you stop using one, check whether you can download your data and ask for it to be deleted

## Questions to ask any health app

Before you hand over a parent's prescriptions and readings, ask:

- Do they clearly ask for consent at sign-up?
- Do they say what they use the data for?
- Can you download your data?
- Can you delete it?
- Is there a way to raise a complaint?

If the answers are hard to find, that tells you something.
