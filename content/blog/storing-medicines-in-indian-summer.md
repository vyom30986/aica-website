It's May in Lucknow and the kitchen feels like a tandoor by noon. On the shelf next to the stove, right above the pressure cooker, sits the family's medicine box: Papa's BP strips, Mummy's thyroid tablets, a cough syrup from last winter and a tube of something nobody can identify. It's convenient. It's also probably the worst spot in the house.

## Why heat and humidity matter

Medicines are chemicals, and many of them are sensitive to where they're kept. Heat, moisture and direct sunlight can affect some medicines, making them less effective or changing how they look or behave.

Most medicine boxes say something like "store in a cool, dry place" or "store below a certain temperature." In an Indian summer, plenty of rooms in the house don't meet that. And monsoon brings humidity on top.

## The usual problem spots

Some common places medicines end up, and why they aren't ideal:

- **Kitchen shelf near the stove.** Heat and steam, every single day
- **Bathroom cabinet.** Humidity from hot showers
- **Windowsill.** Direct sunlight
- **Car dashboard or glovebox.** Gets extremely hot when parked in the sun
- **On top of the fridge.** Warm air from the back of the fridge rises
- **Purse or bag left in the sun.** Easy to forget

Convenience matters, especially for parents who take medicines every day. But there are usually better options in the same house.

## Better places to keep medicines

Look for a spot that's:

- **Away from the stove, geyser and direct sun**
- **Dry**, not near a sink or bathroom
- **Out of reach of children**, ideally in a closed box or drawer
- **Easy for the person taking them** to see and reach

A drawer in a bedroom cupboard often works well. A closed plastic box on a shelf in an inner room is another option. If you use a pill organiser, keep it in the same safe spot, not on the kitchen counter.

## What about the fridge?

Some medicines need refrigeration, for example certain insulins, some eye drops and some syrups once opened. The box or leaflet will say so. If it does, follow it, and ask the pharmacist how to store it properly.

But don't put everything in the fridge "to be safe." Many medicines are not meant to be refrigerated, and fridges can be humid too. Keep medicines in their original packaging, and never in the freezer unless the label says so.

If power cuts are common and you have a medicine that must stay cold, ask the pharmacist what to do in that situation.

## Signs a medicine might not be okay

Check before taking. Ask the pharmacist before using a medicine that:

- Has changed colour or smell
- Has tablets that are crumbling, sticky or swollen
- Has capsules stuck together or soft
- Is a liquid that looks cloudy when it shouldn't, or has separated
- Is past its expiry date

When in doubt, don't use it. Ask the chemist or doctor, and get a replacement if needed.

## A few summer habits that help

- **Keep strips in their boxes.** The foil and box protect them
- **Don't transfer tablets to random containers.** You lose the name, strength and expiry date
- **Carry medicines in an inner bag pocket** when travelling, not in a hot boot
- **Check expiry dates** during your weekly or monthly review
- **Dispose of old medicines properly.** Ask your chemist how to do this in your area

## The takeaway

The kitchen shelf by the stove is handy, but heat and steam aren't kind to medicines. Choose a cool, dry, closed spot away from sun and moisture, follow any fridge instructions on the label, and ask the pharmacist if something looks off.
