"Mummy, dawai li?"
"Haan, le li."

Three words, said quickly, often while she's doing something else. Then a week later you open the pill box and find Tuesday night still sitting there, untouched. She wasn't lying to hurt you. But she also didn't take it.

If you've been on either side of this conversation, you know it's not really about the tablet. It's about what that question feels like to her.

## It's a reflex, not a report

For many parents, "haan, le li" is the same kind of answer as "khana kha liya" or "pahunch gaye." It ends the worry. It's what you say to your child so they can stop asking.

And honestly, she might believe it. Morning and night start to blur when you take the same tablets every day. Did she take it today, or is she remembering yesterday? Most of us couldn't answer that confidently either.

## The reasons underneath

When a parent says yes but didn't, there's usually something real behind it. A few common ones:

- **She forgot, and doesn't want a lecture.** Admitting a miss invites a long call. Saying yes is faster.
- **She doesn't like how the medicine makes her feel.** Maybe it makes her drowsy, or she feels it upsets her stomach. She hasn't told the doctor because "doctor busy hain."
- **She feels fine.** If nothing hurts, the tablet feels optional. This one is especially common with BP and sugar medicines.
- **The strip ran out.** She didn't want to bother anyone to go to the chemist.
- **She feels watched.** Being asked about medicines every day can feel like losing a bit of control over her own body.

None of these are solved by asking the question louder.

## Asking better questions

The trick is to make the true answer as easy to say as the false one.

Instead of "Dawai li?", try something that doesn't have a yes/no escape hatch:

- "Kaunsi wali bachi hai abhi box mein?"
- "Strip mein kitni goliyan reh gayi hain?"
- "Raat wali ke baad neend kaisi aati hai?"

These questions are about the medicine, not about her. They feel less like a test.

And when she does admit she missed one, don't make it a big deal. The way you react to the first honest answer decides whether you get a second one.

## Systems beat memory

Memory is unreliable for everyone, especially with medicines that look the same every day. Some things that help without making anyone feel policed:

**A weekly pill organiser.** Morning, afternoon, night. She can see at a glance whether today's slot is empty. So can whoever is visiting.

**Tie the dose to something she already does.** The morning tablet goes next to the chai cup. The night one sits beside the toothbrush. Habits stick better than alarms.

**A reminder that isn't you.** A phone alarm with a clear label like "BP wali, raat" is less personal than a call from you. She can snooze an alarm without feeling guilty.

**Let her talk to the doctor about side effects.** If she's quietly skipping because of how a medicine feels, that's important information for the doctor. Encourage her to mention it at the next visit, or call sooner. Don't suggest she change or stop anything on her own.

## When you should worry more

A missed dose here and there is common. But if you notice confusion about which tablets are which, or she seems more forgetful than usual in general, mention it to her doctor. And if she ever has chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing or fainting, that's not a medicine conversation. Call 112 or get her to a hospital straight away.

## The takeaway

"Haan, le li" is a kindness, just not a useful one. Ask questions that make honesty easy, set up systems that don't rely on memory, and stay calm when she tells you the truth.
