It's 3:15 pm and you're on a client call, camera on, nodding at a slide. Your phone buzzes face-down on the desk. It's Mummy. Then again. Your stomach drops. You excuse yourself, step out, and call back. "Haan beta, woh lab wale ka number chahiye tha." Not an emergency. But your heart is still racing when you rejoin the call.

If you look after a parent while working full-time, your day has two jobs running in parallel. Only one of them has a calendar.

## The invisible second shift

Caregiving rarely arrives as one big task. It comes in small pieces, scattered through the day:

- Booking the check-up
- Following up on reports
- Remembering the refill
- Calling the chemist
- Explaining the new prescription to whoever is at home
- Answering "kaunsi goli kab leni hai?" for the third time this week

Each piece takes five minutes. Together, they eat your focus. The tiredness isn't from the tasks, it's from always being half on call.

## Batch the boring bits

The single most useful change is moving health admin out of random moments and into a fixed slot.

**Pick one weekly window.** Sunday morning or a weekday lunch. Do refills, appointment bookings and report follow-ups together.

**Keep one running note.** Medicines, dosages as prescribed, doctor names, clinic numbers, last readings. When someone asks, you look it up instead of digging through WhatsApp.

**Front-load refills.** Order or buy before the strip runs out, not when it does. A few days of buffer saves a panicked evening.

**Save useful numbers.** Clinic reception, lab, chemist, a neighbour. Labelled clearly so anyone in the family can find them.

## Split the load honestly

If you have siblings or a partner, the work needs to be divided out loud. Not "help when you can," but specific jobs.

- One person owns appointments
- One owns medicines and refills
- One handles reports and insurance papers

The sibling who lives nearby often ends up with everything by default. If that's you, say so. If it isn't, take on the jobs that can be done remotely, like bookings, online orders and follow-up calls.

## Talk to work, a little

You don't have to share your family's health details with your manager. But a simple line helps: "I look after my parents' health, so occasionally I'll need to step away for a call." Most reasonable managers will appreciate knowing.

Block time for appointments in your calendar the moment they're booked. Treat them like meetings, because they are.

## Lower the number of "urgent" calls

A lot of mid-day calls aren't emergencies. They're questions that could wait, but nobody at home knows that. Agree on a simple rule with your parents:

- **Normal questions:** send a message, you'll reply at lunch or after work
- **Real emergencies:** chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing, fainting. Don't wait to reach you. Call 112 or go to the nearest hospital first, then call

This one agreement can make your workday calmer without making anyone less safe.

## Look after yourself too

It's very easy to treat your own health as the last item on the list. Sleep, food and a little time off are not luxuries. A burnt-out caregiver helps nobody, and parents notice when you're stretched thin. Many of them worry about you just as much.

## The takeaway

You can't remove the second shift, but you can shrink it. Batch the admin, split the work clearly, and agree on what counts as urgent.
