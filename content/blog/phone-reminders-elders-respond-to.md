You set a 9 pm alarm on Papa's phone labelled "Medicine." By the third night, he's learned to swipe it away while still watching the news. "Haan haan, le lunga." He doesn't. The alarm worked perfectly. It just didn't work.

Reminders for elders fail less because of technology and more because of design. A generic beep is easy to ignore. A reminder that fits how your parent actually lives is a lot harder to dismiss.

## Why most reminders get ignored

Think about what a default phone alarm offers: a loud sound, a one-word label and a big "dismiss" button. That's built for waking up, not for taking medicine.

Common reasons elders tune them out:

- The sound is the same as every other alarm, so it means nothing special
- The label doesn't say which tablet, so they have to go and check
- It goes off at a fixed time even when dinner is late
- It's too easy to dismiss without actually acting
- It feels like being nagged by a machine

## Make the reminder specific

"Medicine" is vague. "Raat ki BP wali goli, aadhi tablet, khaane ke baad" is clear. The more the reminder tells them, the less effort it takes to act on it.

Good reminders usually include:

- Which medicine, in the words they use for it
- How much, especially for half tablets
- Before or after food
- A short note if needed, like "paani zyada peena"

If several medicines are due at once, list them together so it's one action, not three alarms.

## Fit their day, not the clock

Most families think in terms of subah, dopahar and raat, not 8:00, 14:00 and 21:00. Indian prescriptions are often written the same way, like 1-0-1 or 1-1-1.

Set reminders around what already happens: after morning chai, after lunch, after dinner. If dinner is usually around 9:30, a 9:00 reminder for an after-food tablet just gets dismissed. Ask your parent when their meals actually happen and set the times accordingly.

## Use a voice they listen to

Some elders respond better to a familiar voice than to a tone. A few options that families find helpful:

- A short voice note from you or a grandchild, set as the alarm sound
- A quick phone call at one important dose time, if you can manage it
- A message in the family group that says "Dadi ne dawai le li?" only if your parent is comfortable with that

Keep it warm. The aim is care, not surveillance.

## Make it easy to confirm

A reminder works best when there's a small action to close it, like tapping "taken." It gives a sense of completion, and it helps both your parent and you know whether the dose actually happened.

If a parent keeps missing a particular dose, don't scold. Ask what's getting in the way. Maybe the tablet causes discomfort, maybe the timing is wrong, maybe they're simply confused about which one it is. Bring it up with the doctor or pharmacist, and don't change or skip doses on your own.

## Keep the physical cues too

Phones help, but they aren't everything. A weekly pill box with morning, afternoon and night sections, kept next to the chai cup or the dinner table, is a powerful reminder on its own. The phone and the pill box together are better than either alone.

## The takeaway

Elders ignore generic alarms, not reminders. Make each one specific, timed to meals, warm in tone and easy to confirm, and keep the pill box where they'll see it.
