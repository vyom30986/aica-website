The prescription says "Review after 15 days." Papa feels fine by day ten. Day fifteen comes and goes. By the time anyone remembers, it's been two months, the strip ran out long ago, and he's been "managing" on half the medicines he started with.

Follow-up visits are probably the most skipped part of Indian healthcare. Not because people don't care, but because once the fever or the pain settles, going back to the clinic feels optional. It isn't.

## Why people skip them

The reasons are usually reasonable on the surface:

- "Ab toh theek lag raha hai, kya zaroorat hai?"
- Another half day off work, another long wait in the clinic
- The consultation fee, again
- Nobody wrote the date down anywhere except the prescription
- The person who took them last time is out of town

Each one makes sense alone. Together, they mean a lot of treatment plans quietly stop halfway.

## What the follow-up is actually for

A first visit is the doctor's best guess based on what they see that day. The follow-up is where they check whether the guess was right. They want to see:

- Is the medicine working, or does it need changing?
- Are there side effects you haven't connected to the medicine?
- Are test results moving in the right direction?
- Can some medicines be reduced or stopped safely?
- Is there anything new that needs attention?

That fourth point surprises people. Sometimes the follow-up is where you get to take fewer medicines, but only the doctor can decide that.

## Chronic conditions need it most

For BP, sugar, thyroid and similar long-term conditions, feeling fine is not a reliable sign that things are under control. These conditions often don't announce themselves until something goes wrong. Regular reviews with readings and tests are how problems get caught early.

Many families fall into a pattern where the same prescription gets refilled at the chemist for a year without anyone seeing the doctor. The chemist can help with the refill, but they can't replace the review. If it's been a long time, it's worth booking one.

## Making follow-ups actually happen

A few practical habits help:

- The moment you get a prescription, put the follow-up date in your phone calendar with a reminder two days before
- If possible, book the next appointment before leaving the clinic
- Note the questions you want to ask as they come up during the weeks in between
- Keep home readings, if you take them, so the visit is useful
- Decide in advance which family member will go, so it doesn't fall through the cracks

If you're managing from another city, share the date in the family group and confirm who is taking Mummy or Papa. "Koi le jayega" usually means nobody will.

## When not to wait for the follow-up

A follow-up date is the latest you should go back, not the earliest. If things get worse, new symptoms appear, or a medicine seems to be causing trouble, call the clinic sooner. And for chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, sudden weakness or confusion, get urgent medical help or call 112.

Feeling better is the reason to go back, not the reason to skip it. The follow-up is where the doctor checks the plan is working, so put the date in your phone the day you get the prescription.
