Thursday night, 10:30 pm. Mummy opens the medicine box, pops the last tablet out of the strip, and says, almost casually, "Yeh wali khatam ho gayi. Kal le aana." Tomorrow is Friday. You have back-to-back meetings. The chemist near home shuts by 10. And this is the sugar medicine, the one she takes twice a day.

Nobody forgot to take the medicine. The medicine simply wasn't there.

## The quiet way doses get missed

When people think about missed doses, they imagine forgetting. But in many families, the bigger problem is supply. The strip runs out, and the gap between "khatam ho gayi" and "le aaye" stretches into a day, then two, then a weekend.

It rarely feels dramatic. It just slips. And because nothing obvious happens after one or two missed days, it can happen again next month.

## Where the refill chain breaks

Refills go wrong in small, ordinary ways:

- **Nobody's counting.** You know she takes it, but not how many are left
- **Different strips run out on different days.** Five medicines, five different end dates
- **The prescription has expired** or the chemist needs a new one, and the next doctor's visit is two weeks away
- **The chemist is out of stock** of that specific medicine
- **The person who usually buys it is away,** travelling, unwell or busy
- **Everyone assumes someone else ordered it,** the classic family group problem

Each of these is fixable. The trick is catching them before the last tablet.

## Count forward, not backward

The simplest habit: work out when a strip will end, not just notice when it has.

Say a strip has 10 tablets and the dose is 1-0-1. That's two a day, so it lasts five days. If you have two strips, that's ten days. Mark the date. Set a reminder for three or four days before it.

A few practical tips:

- **Check all strips on the same day each week.** Sunday works for many families
- **Note the count on paper or your phone.** "Sugar wali: 14 left, ends around 20th"
- **Keep a small buffer** so a delay of a day or two doesn't create a gap, but don't stockpile more than you need, especially for medicines that may change
- **Watch for half tablets.** A "½-0-½" dose means one tablet lasts a full day. Count accordingly

## Fix the prescription side early

Some medicines need a valid prescription each time. If the last prescription is old, or the chemist needs a fresh one, that can block the refill.

Keep a clear photo of the current prescription handy. When a check-up is coming, plan it before the medicines run out, not after. And if the doctor changes a dose, update your count, because the strip will now last a different number of days.

## Make the chemist part of the system

A good neighbourhood chemist can be your best refill partner. Many will:

- Keep a regular medicine aside if you tell them in advance
- Let you know if something is out of stock, so you can plan
- Remember what your parents usually buy

Give them a call a few days before the refill is due. It's much easier than a late-night scramble.

## If you've already run out

If a regular medicine has run out and you can't get it immediately, call the chemist or doctor and ask what to do. Don't double up on the next dose when you get it, unless you're specifically told to. And don't borrow tablets from someone else's prescription. If someone feels unwell in a way that worries you, especially with chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing or fainting, call 112 or get to a hospital.

## The takeaway

Most missed doses in families aren't forgetfulness, they're empty strips. Count forward, set a reminder before the end date, and keep the chemist in the loop.
