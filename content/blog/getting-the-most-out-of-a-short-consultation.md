You waited two hours in the clinic. Your name is finally called. You walk in, sit down, and the doctor is already writing. Four minutes later you're back outside holding a prescription, and only then do you remember the one question you actually came to ask.

Short consultations are a reality in most Indian clinics. Doctors see a lot of patients, and that isn't changing overnight. But you can make those few minutes count for a lot more.

## Know your one main question

Before you go in, decide the single most important thing you want from this visit. Is it "why is Mummy's sugar still high?" or "is this cough something to worry about?" or "can these two medicines be taken together?"

Say it first, not last. Doctors tend to focus on whatever you open with. If the main worry comes out as you're leaving, there's often no time left to deal with it properly.

## Write it down before you leave home

Memory is unreliable in a busy clinic, and it gets worse when you're anxious or managing a parent at the same time. A short note on your phone fixes that.

Keep it to a few lines:

- The main problem, and since when
- Any change since the last visit (better, worse, new symptoms)
- Current medicines, including anything from another doctor
- Two or three questions, in order of importance

Reading from your phone isn't rude. Most doctors appreciate a patient who is organised.

## Bring the evidence

A lot of consultation time is lost to "aapki last report kahan hai?" followed by frantic scrolling through WhatsApp. Have these ready before you walk in:

- The previous prescription
- Recent test reports, in date order
- Your actual medicine strips or a photo of them
- Home readings, if you check BP or sugar at home

Even a simple list of dates and numbers is far more useful than "BP thoda upar neeche hota hai."

## Ask the questions that prevent confusion later

Before you stand up, check you understand the plan. A few quick questions can save a lot of trouble at home:

- What is each medicine for?
- When and how should it be taken: before or after food?
- How long should we continue it?
- What should make us come back sooner?
- When is the next follow-up?

If anything is unclear, ask the doctor or the staff to repeat it. "Ek baar phir se samjha dijiye" is a perfectly reasonable thing to say.

## Don't save the scary symptom for later

Sometimes people mention something serious only at the very end, almost as an afterthought. "Haan, aur kabhi kabhi chest mein dard hota hai." That should be the first thing you say, not the last.

And if someone has sudden chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, sudden weakness on one side or confusion, don't wait for the next appointment at all. Get urgent medical help or call 112.

## After the visit

The consultation doesn't end at the door. Take a clear photo of the prescription right away. Read it once while you're still in the clinic, so you can ask the staff if a word is unreadable. At the chemist, confirm each medicine and its timing again.

Then set reminders on the same day, while it's fresh. It's much easier than trying to reconstruct the plan from memory next week.

## The takeaway

You can't make the consultation longer, but you can make it sharper. Lead with your main worry, bring your papers and readings, and leave only when the plan is clear. Four well-used minutes beat fifteen confused ones.
