Mrs Verma buys her thyroid tablets from the same shop near her house every month. One month she is visiting her daughter in Kanpur, the strip runs out, and she buys from a shop there. Next month she orders online because her daughter set it up. Nobody at her old counter even noticed she had gone.

That is how most chemists lose regular customers. Not with a fight, not with a complaint. Just a quiet gap that nobody filled.

## Loyalty programmes don't really fit chemists

Points, cards and cashback work for shopping malls. For medicines they feel odd. Nobody wants to collect points on their diabetes tablets, and most small shops can't afford big discounts anyway.

What people actually value is simpler. They want their medicine ready when they need it, and they want someone to remember them. A refill reminder does both.

## What a refill reminder actually is

It is a short message sent a few days before a patient's regular medicine is likely to run out. Something like:

"Namaste aunty, aapki BP ki dawai ka strip agle hafte tak khatam ho sakta hai. Ready rakh dein?"

That's it. No sales pitch. It does three useful things at once:

- The patient doesn't run out, which is good for their health
- The family doesn't have to remember, which is a relief
- The shop gets the repeat sale it would otherwise have lost

## Why it builds real loyalty

When the reminder comes from the same counter every month, the patient starts to feel looked after. The son working in Bengaluru who manages Papa's medicines from far away knows the local chemist is keeping an eye out. That is a relationship, not a transaction.

It also changes the shop's day. Instead of guessing what stock to keep, you have a rough idea of who is coming back and when. Fewer "kal aana, abhi nahi hai" moments.

## Doing it by hand (and where it breaks)

Plenty of chemists already do a version of this in their heads. Uncle knows Sharma ji comes on the first of the month. The trouble starts when the list grows.

A simple manual setup can work for a while:

- A register with the patient's name, phone number and regular medicines
- The date of the last purchase and how many days the pack covers
- A weekly check to see who is due, followed by a WhatsApp message

It breaks when the shop gets busy, when the person who keeps the register is on leave, or when doses change and nobody updates the page. That's where some simple software help makes a real difference.

## Getting it right

A few ground rules keep reminders welcome instead of annoying:

- Ask permission first. Only message people who are happy to hear from you
- Keep it short and polite, and don't push extra products
- Never suggest changing a dose or skipping a medicine. If the patient has a question, point them to their doctor
- If someone has stopped a medicine on their doctor's advice, update your records and stop the reminders

Respect is the whole point. A reminder that feels like spam undoes the trust it was meant to build.

## The bigger picture

Chronic medicines for BP, sugar, thyroid and cholesterol are taken for months or years. For a neighbourhood shop, these patients are the steady base of the business. Keeping them is far cheaper than finding new ones, and it is genuinely good for the patient too.

## The takeaway

The best loyalty programme for a chemist is a message that arrives just before the strip runs out. It costs almost nothing, keeps patients on track, and keeps them coming back to your counter.
