It's 9:40 pm in Bengaluru. You've just shut your laptop, and you call home. Papa picks up on the fourth ring, the TV loud in the background. "Haan beta, sab theek." You ask if he took his BP tablet. A pause. "Morning wala? Haan." You ask about the night one. Another pause, slightly longer. You both know what that pause means.

If you manage a parent's medicines from another city, you know this call. You are the reminder, the pharmacy tracker and the worried voice, all from 1,800 km away, all through a phone line that cuts out whenever Papa walks to the balcony.

## The job nobody gave you a title for

Nobody sat you down and said, "You're now in charge of Papa's blood pressure." It just happened. Maybe you were the one who read the prescription out loud at the clinic. Maybe you're the sibling with the steadiest job, or the one who answers the phone.

Now you hold a mental list that looks something like this:

- Which tablets he takes, and when
- Which ones are "1-0-1" and which are morning only
- When the strip will run out
- When the next check-up is due
- What the last BP reading was, and whether anyone wrote it down

That's a lot to carry in your head while also doing your actual job.

## What goes wrong from far away

Distance doesn't make people careless. It makes information slow. You hear about things after they've happened, usually in passing. "Oh, woh wali goli khatam ho gayi thi, two days ago." By the time you know, the gap has already happened.

The other problem is that Papa is an adult with his own routine and his own pride. He doesn't want to be quizzed like a schoolboy every evening. So the calls get shorter, the answers get vaguer, and you start guessing.

Guessing is the real enemy here. You can't help with what you can't see.

## A setup that works better than nightly interrogations

You don't need surveillance. You need a few simple systems that reduce how much depends on memory, yours and his.

**Get the prescription in writing, in one place.** Ask someone at home to send you a clear photo of the latest prescription. Not the one from last year. Keep it somewhere you can find it in ten seconds.

**Use a pill organiser at home.** A weekly box with morning and night compartments does a lot of quiet work. Whoever is at home, Papa, Mummy or a helper, can fill it on Sunday. An empty compartment is easier to check than a memory.

**Track the refill date, not just the medicine.** Count how many tablets are left in the strip and work out roughly when it ends. Put a reminder a few days before that date. Most missed doses in families aren't forgetfulness. They're "the strip ran out and nobody went to the chemist."

**Ask for the reading, not the feeling.** "How are you feeling?" gets you "theek hoon." If there's a BP machine at home, "What did the machine say this morning?" gets you a number. Keep a simple log of those numbers to show the doctor at the next visit.

**Make the local chemist an ally.** If there's a chemist your family trusts, they often remember what Papa buys. A quick call to them can sometimes tell you more than a week of family calls.

## When to stop managing and act

Most days, this is a quiet, boring job. That's good. But know the signs that are not a "we'll discuss on Sunday" situation. Chest pain, sudden weakness on one side, confusion, slurred speech, severe headache, fainting or trouble breathing all need urgent medical help. Tell whoever is at home to call 112 or get him to a hospital right away, then call you.

And if Papa tells you he's stopped a tablet because he "feels fine now," don't argue on the phone. Ask him to speak to his doctor before changing anything, and offer to join that call.

You can't be in the room every day, but you can make the system less dependent on memory and guesswork.
