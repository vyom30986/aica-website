"Doctor sahab, bas thoda weakness hai. Aur kabhi kabhi gas. Aur neend bhi theek nahi aati." The doctor nods, asks two questions, and writes something. Walking out, you wonder whether they understood what was actually going on. Honestly, you're not sure you explained it.

Describing symptoms is harder than it sounds. Words like "weakness," "gas" and "BP low lag raha hai" mean different things to different people. A little structure helps the doctor understand you faster and more accurately.

## Start with the main thing

Lead with the symptom that worries you most, and say it plainly. Not a list of five things at once. "Mujhe teen din se chest ke beech mein bhaari lag raha hai" is a much better opening than "sab theek hai, bas thoda yeh, thoda woh."

Once the main one is clear, you can add the smaller things.

## Answer the questions before they're asked

Doctors are trying to fill in a few specific blanks. If you cover these, you've done most of the work for them:

- **When did it start?** A date or "since last Tuesday" beats "kaafi time se"
- **Where exactly?** Point to the spot. Does it move or spread anywhere?
- **What does it feel like?** Sharp, dull, burning, pressing, throbbing, tight
- **How bad is it?** On a scale of 1 to 10, and is it getting better or worse
- **How often?** Constant, comes and goes, only at certain times
- **What triggers it?** Food, walking, stairs, lying down, stress
- **What helps?** Rest, a position, a home remedy, a medicine you tried

You don't need medical words. Your own words with these details are perfect.

## Replace vague words with real examples

"Weakness" is the classic vague word. It could mean tiredness, breathlessness, muscle weakness, dizziness or low mood. Instead, describe what you can't do: "Sabzi lene jaata hoon toh beech mein rukna padta hai" or "Seedhi chadhte waqt saans phoolti hai."

Same with "gas." Is it bloating, burping, burning in the chest, pain after eating? A specific example tells the doctor far more than the label.

## Mention what you've already tried

Did you take a painkiller, an antacid, a home remedy, or a leftover medicine from last time? Say so, along with whether it helped. It's useful information, and it also matters for safety when the doctor prescribes something new.

## Keep a quick symptom diary

For symptoms that come and go, memory is unreliable. A few lines in your phone each time it happens helps a lot:

- Date and time
- What happened and how long it lasted
- What you were doing just before
- Any reading you took, like BP or sugar

Showing the doctor a week of notes is often more useful than any amount of explaining.

## When describing isn't the priority

Some symptoms shouldn't wait for an appointment at all. Chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, sudden weakness or numbness on one side, slurred speech, confusion or fainting need urgent medical help. Call 112 or go to the nearest emergency.

If you're describing symptoms on behalf of a parent, let them speak first, then add what you've noticed. You often see changes they don't mention.

## The takeaway

Lead with the main worry, then answer when, where, how bad, how often and what helps. Swap vague words for real examples, and keep short notes. Your doctor can only work with what you tell them.
