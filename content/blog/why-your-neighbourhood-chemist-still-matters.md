You've ordered phone chargers, groceries and a birthday cake online this month. So when Papa's BP medicine runs low, ordering it online feels like the obvious next step. Then Papa says, "Nahi, Gupta ji ki dukaan se le aana. Woh jaante hain." And you wonder if that's just habit, or if he has a point.

He might have a point.

## What the counter gives you that a cart doesn't

Online ordering is genuinely useful. It's convenient, it can help when you're far away, and for some people it's the only practical option. This isn't about choosing one side forever.

But a neighbourhood chemist offers some things that are hard to replace:

- **A face that knows your family.** They often remember what your parents usually buy, and notice when something looks different
- **Immediate availability.** When the strip runs out at 8 pm, the counter is ten minutes away
- **Quick questions answered.** "Before food or after?" gets a reply in seconds, not an email
- **Handwriting translation.** An experienced chemist is often very good at reading a doctor's scribble, and will call the clinic if it's unclear
- **Local knowledge.** Which nearby doctor is in on Sundays, which lab is open late

## The relationship is the feature

The real value of a neighbourhood chemist is continuity. When the same person sees your family's prescriptions over months or years, they can sometimes spot things that a one-time order won't.

Say Mummy starts buying a painkiller often. A chemist who knows her might gently ask if she's seen the doctor about it. Or say two different doctors prescribe medicines that a pharmacist would want to double-check together. Someone who sees both prescriptions is in a better position to notice.

That kind of attention depends on you going back to the same counter.

## Where neighbourhood chemists struggle

It's fair to say the local model has its challenges:

- **Stock limits.** A small store can't keep everything. Some medicines need to be ordered in
- **Everything lives in memory or a register.** If the regular person is away, the next person may not know your history
- **No reminders.** The chemist can't easily call every customer before their strip runs out
- **Busy hours.** In the evening rush, there's less time for questions

None of this is a reason to give up on them. It's a reason to work with them more deliberately.

## How to get the most from your local chemist

A few habits that make the relationship work better:

- **Stick to one store** for your family's regular medicines, as far as possible
- **Tell them all the medicines** a parent takes, even ones from other doctors, so they can flag anything worth checking
- **Call ahead for refills** a few days before you run out, so they can arrange stock
- **Share a clear prescription photo** if you're ordering on someone's behalf from another city
- **Ask questions.** Pharmacists are trained for this. But for decisions about starting, stopping or changing a dose, they'll send you to the doctor, and they should

## Online and offline can work together

The best setup for many families is a mix. A trusted local chemist for regular medicines and quick help. Online options when you're travelling or something isn't in stock nearby. What matters most is that someone has the full picture of what your parent takes.

## The takeaway

Your neighbourhood chemist isn't just a shop. It's a person who remembers your family, reads the handwriting and answers the 8 pm question. Go back to the same counter, keep them informed and treat them like part of the care team.
