You said one sentence. "Papa, aap apni BP ki dawai regularly le rahe ho na?" And somehow, within thirty seconds, it became a conversation about how he raised you, how he's been managing his own life for sixty years, and how you young people think you know everything.

The medicine never even came up again.

Many Indian dads don't love being asked about their health by their children. But it's possible to have the conversation without it turning into a fight. It just needs a different approach.

## Why it gets heated so fast

For a lot of fathers, being the one who takes care of things is part of who they are. A question about his medicines can sound, to him, like "I don't think you can manage yourself anymore."

There's also the plain fact that nobody likes being told what to do. You'd bristle too if he asked whether you'd drunk enough water today, every day.

So the goal isn't to win. It's to make the conversation feel like teamwork, not a correction.

## Pick the moment carefully

Timing does half the work.

- **Not during an argument about something else.** It'll get pulled into the old fight.
- **Not in front of relatives.** Health is private, and an audience makes people defensive.
- **Not right after a scare,** when everyone is emotional. Wait a day or two.
- **Yes during something side by side.** A drive, a walk, sitting on the terrace. Talking while doing something together feels less like a confrontation.

## Lead with curiosity, not instructions

Questions work better than statements. Compare:

- "You have to take it at night." versus "Raat wali kab lete ho usually?"
- "You're not taking it properly." versus "Koi dawai hai jo aapko theek nahi lagti?"
- "Doctor said so." versus "Doctor ne last time kya bola tha iske baare mein?"

When he explains his routine, he's the expert again. You'll also learn things you didn't know. Maybe he skips the afternoon dose because he's out. Maybe one tablet makes him feel low. That's useful information, and it should go to his doctor.

## Make it about you, a little

Sometimes the honest version lands best. "Papa, main door hoon, toh thoda tension hota hai. Bas yeh jaanna tha ki sab theek chal raha hai." You're not accusing him. You're telling him how you feel.

You can also ask for his help. "Can you send me a photo of the prescription? I want to keep a copy just in case." It's a small, reasonable request, and it gives him a role.

## What not to do

A few things that reliably backfire:

- Quoting forwards or videos about medicines
- Comparing him to other uncles ("Verma uncle toh sab time pe leta hai")
- Suggesting he change, stop or adjust a dose. That's a conversation for him and his doctor or pharmacist, never for you to decide
- Bringing it up every single call

If he wants to change something, encourage him to raise it with his doctor. Offer to come along or join the call.

## Have a plan for the big stuff

There's one area where you can be firm without being preachy. Agree together, calmly, that if he ever has chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing or faints, he or whoever is with him will call 112 or go to the hospital right away. That's not nagging. It's a family plan.

## The takeaway

Ask, don't instruct. Pick a calm moment, let him explain his own routine, and leave dose decisions to his doctor. Keep it short and come back to it another day if needed. A conversation that ends well is worth more than one that ends with you being right.
