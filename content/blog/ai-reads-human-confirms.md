The obvious way to build a prescription app is to let the AI do everything. Snap a photo, the machine reads it, the reminders appear. Fast, magical, done. I could build it that way. I decided not to.

In AICA, the AI reads the prescription, but a human always confirms it against the photo before anything is saved. Here's why that one extra step matters so much to me.

## Handwriting is hard, even for good AI

Anyone who has looked at an Indian prescription knows the problem. Hurried handwriting, abbreviations, a stamp over half the page, sometimes a photo taken in bad light at the clinic door. AI that reads handwriting has improved a lot, and it's genuinely useful. But it still makes mistakes.

And some of those mistakes are the dangerous kind. Medicine names can look very similar on paper. A dose pattern like 1-0-1 can be misread as 1-1-1. A half tablet can turn into a whole one. In most apps, a small error is an inconvenience. In a medicine app, it can be a real risk.

## Confident is not the same as correct

The tricky thing about AI is that it often sounds sure of itself even when it's wrong. It doesn't hesitate or squint the way a person does. If the app quietly saved whatever the AI guessed, nobody would notice the error until it caused a problem.

So the design rule is simple: the AI suggests, the person decides.

## What the confirm step looks like

When you scan a prescription in AICA, you see two things side by side: the original photo, and what the AI thinks it says. Before anything is saved, you check them against each other.

- Is the medicine name right?
- Is the strength right?
- Is the timing right, morning, afternoon, night?
- Is the quantity right, including half tablets?
- Is the duration right?

If something looks off, you correct it. If you're unsure, the right move is to ask the doctor or the chemist, not to guess. Only after a person confirms does it become the record that drives reminders and refills.

## The same rule at the chemist counter

For pharmacies, AICA reads the doctor's handwriting to save time at a busy counter. But the chemist stays in charge. Their years of experience reading local doctors' writing are exactly what makes the confirm step strong. The AI handles the first pass. The chemist's judgement handles the final word.

I think of it as a good assistant, not a replacement. Software is fast. People are careful. Together they're better than either alone.

## Yes, it adds a few seconds

The honest trade-off is that confirming takes a little time. Some people will find it slower than a fully automatic app. I'm okay with that.

A few extra seconds while the prescription is right in front of you is much cheaper than a wrong reminder running for weeks. And it keeps the person aware of what they're taking, which is useful in itself.

## What this says about AICA

This one decision reflects how I want to build everything. AICA doesn't give medical advice, doesn't change doses, and doesn't pretend to know more than it does. It helps you keep track of what your doctor prescribed, clearly and correctly. We're early, with an interactive prototype and a waitlist at aicaglobal.in, and pilots starting in Lucknow.

## The takeaway

AI is great at reading fast. Humans are better at catching what matters. In AICA, the AI reads the prescription and a person always confirms it against the photo, because with medicines, getting it right beats getting it instantly.
