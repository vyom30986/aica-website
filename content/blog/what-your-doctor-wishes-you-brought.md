The doctor asks, "Which medicines are you taking right now?" Papa says, "Woh safed wali, aur ek chhoti si gol." Everyone in the room knows that isn't going to help. The next five minutes are spent guessing.

Doctors rarely say it out loud, but there's a short list of things that would make every visit faster, safer and more useful. None of it is complicated. Most of it fits in one folder or one phone album.

## Every medicine you take, not just theirs

This is the big one. Doctors need to know everything a person is taking, including:

- Medicines from other doctors or specialists
- Anything bought over the counter, like painkillers or antacids
- Vitamins, supplements and ayurvedic or homeopathic medicines
- Eye drops, inhalers and creams, which people often forget count

The easiest way is to bring the actual strips and bottles in a bag, or a clear photo of each. A written list with the name, strength and how often you take it works too. Some combinations don't go well together, and the doctor can only check what they know about.

## The previous prescription

Even if it's from the same doctor, bring it. It shows what was planned last time, what dose was started and when. It also helps the doctor see whether the plan was followed, which matters more than most people think.

If you've seen more than one doctor, bring the latest prescription from each. Keep them in date order.

## Reports, in order

Blood tests, scans, ECGs and discharge summaries tell the story of a person's health. A pile of unsorted papers, or forty forwarded PDFs in a WhatsApp chat, makes that story hard to read.

A few minutes of sorting helps a lot:

- Put reports in date order, latest on top
- Keep older reports for comparison, they show trends
- If you have a hospital discharge summary, always bring it

## Home readings with dates

If you check BP or sugar at home, write the readings down with the date and time. "Morning fasting 132, 14 November" is useful. "Sugar thoda badha hua tha" is not.

A simple table on paper or in your phone is enough. Note anything unusual alongside, like "felt dizzy" or "missed dinner." Patterns matter more than single numbers.

## A short note on what's changed

What's different since the last visit? New symptoms, a fall, a change in appetite or sleep, side effects you suspect, a medicine that was hard to take. Write these down before you leave home, because they're easy to forget once you're sitting in front of the doctor.

## Someone who can listen

For older parents especially, bringing a family member helps. One person talks to the doctor, the other listens and takes notes. Two sets of ears catch more than one, and it's easier to remember instructions later.

You don't need to bring printouts of things you read online, though it's fine to ask a question about them. Clear, short and in order is what helps.
