Mummy is standing in the kitchen at 8 am, tablet in one hand, aloo paratha on the tawa. She looks at the strip, then at you. "Yeh khaane se pehle thi ya baad mein?" Nobody remembers. The prescription is in a drawer somewhere. She takes it with the first bite and hopes that counts.

It's one of the most common medicine questions in any Indian home. And there's a reason doctors bother to write it down.

## Why food timing matters at all

When you take a medicine, your body has to absorb it, usually through the stomach and intestines. What's in your stomach at that moment can change how that happens.

For some medicines, food helps. For others, food gets in the way. And for many, it doesn't make much difference. The doctor or pharmacist knows which group each medicine falls into, which is why the instruction is on the prescription.

## Common reasons behind "after food"

Some medicines can irritate the stomach lining when taken on an empty stomach. Certain painkillers are a common example. Taking them after a meal can make them gentler on the stomach.

Some medicines are also absorbed better when there's food present. And for some medicines used for diabetes, timing them around meals is part of how they're meant to work, because they're working with the food you eat.

## Common reasons behind "before food"

Some medicines are absorbed better on an empty stomach. Food can slow them down or reduce how much the body takes in. Certain thyroid medicines and some antacid-type medicines are often given this way, but your own prescription is what counts.

Some medicines are meant to act before the meal arrives, like ones that prepare the stomach.

"Before food" usually means some time before eating, not one bite earlier. The exact gap depends on the medicine. If the prescription doesn't say, ask the pharmacist how long before.

## What "with food" and "empty stomach" mean

The wording can be confusing, so here's a rough guide:

- **Before food (AC):** taken before a meal. Ask how long before
- **After food (PC):** taken after eating, usually soon after the meal
- **With food:** taken during or right around the meal
- **Empty stomach:** typically means not having eaten for a while. Ask the pharmacist what that means for this medicine
- **No instruction:** don't assume. Ask, because "doesn't matter" is also an answer, but it should come from them

## Making it stick at home

Knowing the rule is the easy part. Remembering it every day is harder, especially when someone takes four or five medicines.

**Ask the chemist to write it on the box.** "Khane ke baad" in big letters on the strip box saves a lot of guessing.

**Group medicines by timing.** Keep "before breakfast" medicines in one spot and "after dinner" ones in another. A pill organiser with morning and night slots helps.

**Link it to the meal routine.** If breakfast is always after puja, the before-food tablet can go right after puja.

**Write a simple chart.** One line per medicine: name, morning/afternoon/night, before or after food. Stick it on the fridge.

## If it's been taken the "wrong" way

Most of the time, taking one dose at the wrong food timing is not a big emergency. But don't try to fix it by taking another dose. If it keeps happening, or you're worried, ask the pharmacist or doctor what to do. If someone feels very unwell after a medicine, with trouble breathing, swelling of the face, chest pain or fainting, get urgent medical help or call 112.

## The takeaway

"Before food" and "after food" are there for a reason: food changes how some medicines work. Get the instruction written clearly, group medicines by timing, and ask the pharmacist whenever it's not written. Guessing at the tawa isn't a system.
