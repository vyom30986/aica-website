The chemist uncle hands over the strip and says, "Yeh wala le lo, same hai, sasta padega." Mummy hesitates. It's a different name, a different colour box and much cheaper. "Same kaise ho sakta hai? Doctor ne toh woh wala likha tha." She looks at you. You don't know either.

It's one of the most common doubts at an Indian medicine counter. Let's clear it up.

## What a medicine's "name" actually means

Every medicine has an active ingredient, the actual chemical that does the work. That's its generic name. Paracetamol, metformin and amlodipine are examples of generic names.

Companies then sell that same ingredient under their own brand names, with their own packaging. So one active ingredient can be on the shelf under many different names, at many different prices.

When people say "generic medicine," they usually mean a version sold by its ingredient name, or a lower-priced brand, rather than a well-known, heavily marketed one.

## Is the generic the same as the branded one?

In principle, a generic version is meant to have the same active ingredient, in the same strength, and work the same way as the branded version. That's what makes it a substitute.

But "same ingredient" isn't the whole story. Medicines can differ in:

- **Strength.** 5 mg is not 10 mg. Always check the number
- **Form.** A regular tablet and a slow-release one with the same ingredient are not interchangeable
- **Combination.** Some products combine two or more ingredients. A single-ingredient version isn't the same thing
- **Inactive ingredients** like fillers or colours, which matter if someone has a specific allergy

This is why swapping should always go through someone qualified, not guesswork.

## Why prices vary so much

The price difference often comes from things like brand marketing, packaging and distribution, not necessarily from the medicine itself. That's why the same ingredient can cost very different amounts.

India also has government schemes and stores that focus on affordable generic medicines. If cost is a worry, these are worth asking about.

## How to switch safely

If you'd like a cheaper option, there's a sensible way to do it:

- **Ask the doctor** if a generic or lower-cost version is fine for this medicine. Some doctors write the generic name directly
- **Ask the pharmacist** to confirm the substitute has the same active ingredient, strength and form
- **Check the strip yourself.** Compare the ingredient name and the mg on both
- **Tell the family.** If the box looks different, Mummy or Papa may think it's a new medicine and get confused, or take both
- **Stick with one version** once you've switched, so the box doesn't keep changing

## When to be extra careful

For some medicines, doctors prefer patients to stay on the same product consistently. Certain thyroid, seizure or heart medicines are sometimes in this group, but it depends on the person. If your parent takes medicines for long-term conditions, ask the doctor before switching between brands or generics.

And never switch to save money by cutting the dose, skipping tablets or splitting strips across family members. If cost is the issue, say so to the doctor. There are often options.

If a parent feels unwell after changing to a different version, tell the doctor. For severe symptoms like chest pain, swelling of the face, trouble breathing or fainting, call 112 or get urgent medical help.

## The takeaway

Generic and branded versions can contain the same active ingredient, but strength, form and combination matter. Ask the doctor or pharmacist before switching, check the label, and tell the family when the box changes.
