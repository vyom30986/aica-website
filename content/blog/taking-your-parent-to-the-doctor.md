You've taken a half day off. Papa has put on his good shirt and is insisting he's "bilkul theek." In the car he tells you not to mention the knee pain, the dizziness, or the fact that he stopped one of his tablets last week because it "made him feel heavy." You're about to walk into the clinic with a patient who doesn't want to be one.

Taking a parent to the doctor is a role reversal nobody trains us for. It's part driver, part secretary, part translator, and part diplomat. Here's how to do it well without making your parent feel small.

## Before the visit: talk, don't interrogate

A day or two before, sit with them and ask what's been bothering them. Keep it casual. Over chai works better than a checklist.

Then make a short list together:

- What they want to ask the doctor
- Any new symptoms, falls, sleep or appetite changes
- Any medicine they've had trouble with, or stopped, or forgot

That last point matters. If a parent has stopped or changed a medicine on their own, the doctor needs to know. Don't scold, just make sure it gets said. Only the doctor should decide what happens next.

## Pack the bag

Put everything in one folder or bag the night before:

- All current medicines, or clear photos of each strip
- The last prescription from every doctor they see
- Recent reports, sorted by date
- Home BP or sugar readings, if you keep them
- Their glasses and hearing aid, if they use them

Older patients often struggle to read small print or hear quietly spoken instructions. Small things like glasses make a big difference in the room.

## In the room: let them speak first

It's tempting to take over. You know the history, you're faster, the doctor is busy. But your parent is the patient. Let them describe how they feel in their own words, then fill in the gaps.

A good rhythm is:

- Parent explains the main problem
- You add the facts: dates, readings, medicines
- You gently raise anything they skipped. "Papa, woh chakkar wali baat bhi bata dijiye"

Talk to the doctor with your parent, not about them. Nobody likes being discussed as if they're not there.

## Take notes like a professional

You will not remember everything. Write down the diagnosis, every medicine, the timing (like 1-0-1 or after food), how long to continue, what tests to do, and when to come back.

Ask the doctor what warning signs should make you come back sooner. And remember the basic rule: chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness, confusion or fainting means get urgent medical help or call 112, not wait for the next appointment.

## After: the follow-through is the real work

The visit is maybe ten minutes. The weeks after are where things actually go right or wrong.

- Photograph the prescription before leaving the clinic
- At the chemist, check each medicine against the prescription
- Explain the new routine to your parent, and to whoever lives with them
- Set reminders that day
- Put the follow-up date in your calendar, not just theirs

If you live in another city, share the updated plan with the sibling or relative who's nearby. One clear message beats five confused ones in the family group.
