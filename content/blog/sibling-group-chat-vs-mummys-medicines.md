The family WhatsApp group is called something like "Sharma Siblings" or "Home Team", and on most days it's memes, wedding photos and someone asking for the Netflix password. Then one evening, a message lands: "Mummy ki sugar wali dawai khatam ho gayi kya? Kisi ne order ki?"

Three blue ticks. Two thumbs-up reactions. Nobody actually ordered it.

## The "someone will do it" problem

When three siblings share responsibility, it often becomes nobody's responsibility. Everyone cares. Everyone assumes someone else has it covered. The sibling in Lucknow thinks the one in Pune is ordering online. The one in Pune thinks the Lucknow sibling will just walk to the chemist.

Meanwhile, Mummy quietly stretches the last few tablets, or skips them, because she doesn't want to "trouble the children."

Group chats are great for feelings. They're bad at ownership.

## Why health info gets lost in the chat

Think about where Mummy's medical information currently lives:

- A prescription photo from March, buried under 400 forwards
- A lab report PDF someone shared, with no message explaining it
- A voice note from Papa about what the doctor said
- A message that says "doctor ne dose change kiya hai" with no detail

When someone needs the current list of medicines, they scroll, give up, and call whoever went to the last appointment. That person may not remember either.

## Decide who owns what

You don't need a family meeting with slides. You need a few clear roles, said out loud once.

**The medicines owner.** One person knows the current list and keeps the latest prescription handy. When anything changes, it goes through them.

**The refills owner.** One person tracks when strips will run out and makes sure they're bought in time. Often this is the sibling who lives closest, or whoever has a good relationship with the local chemist.

**The appointments owner.** One person books check-ups, remembers follow-ups and goes along, or arranges someone to.

Roles can be shared or rotated. What matters is that each job has a name next to it. "Sab dekh lenge" is not a plan.

## Rules that keep the group useful

A few small habits make a big difference:

- **Pin the current prescription.** Not the old one. When it changes, unpin and replace.
- **Say "done" when something is done.** "Ordered the BP strip, coming tomorrow" stops the next person from double-buying.
- **Keep health messages short and specific.** Medicine name, dose, timing, who's handling it.
- **Never pass dose changes as a forward.** If a doctor changes a dose, the person who was there should write it clearly and attach the new prescription. Nobody should adjust Mummy's tablets based on a half-remembered message.

## Respect the person in the middle

It's easy to forget that Mummy is part of this too. She doesn't want to feel like a project being managed by her children. Include her in decisions. Ask before sharing her reports with the whole extended family. Some parents are fine with everyone knowing; others would rather keep their sugar numbers between them and one child.

And if the sibling nearby is doing most of the physical work, the chemist runs, the clinic visits, the late-night calls, acknowledge that. Distance shouldn't mean the burden lands on one person by default.

## When the chat isn't enough

For anything urgent, the group chat is the wrong tool. If Mummy has chest pain, sudden weakness, confusion, trouble breathing or faints, whoever is with her should call 112 or get to a hospital first. Messages can come later.

## The takeaway

The group chat can stay for love and memes. Medicines need an owner, a current prescription and a clear "done."
