Dadi's sugar tablet is on the dining table. Chachi thinks Bhaiya gave it to her after breakfast. Bhaiya thinks Chachi did. Dadi, asked directly, says "haan, le li thi," but she isn't sure if that was today or yesterday. Four adults in one house, and nobody actually knows.

Joint families are wonderful for care. There's always someone at home, always someone to take Dadu for a walk or sit with him at the clinic. But more people also means more room for confusion, especially around medicines.

## The good part, first

A joint family is a care system most nuclear families would envy. Someone notices when Dadi isn't eating well. Someone can always go to the chemist. Responsibilities are shared, and nobody has to manage alone.

The challenge is coordination. When everyone is a little bit responsible, it's easy for nobody to be fully responsible.

## Where things slip

Some situations come up in almost every big household:

- Two people give the same dose, or both assume the other did
- Medicines for different elders get kept together and mixed up
- One relative takes a parent to a new doctor and the old prescription stays on the fridge
- The person who knows all the timings travels for a week
- Several people share one phone, and the reminders go to whoever has it in their pocket

None of this is carelessness. It's what happens when information lives in people's heads instead of one shared place.

## Make one person the anchor

The simplest fix is to name one person as the primary caregiver for each elder's medicines, and one backup. Others can still help, but the anchor is the one who:

- Keeps the current medicine list updated
- Gives or checks the doses
- Handles refills with the chemist
- Attends or follows up on doctor visits

It doesn't have to be the same person forever. Just make sure everyone knows who it is this month.

## Keep medicines separate and labelled

If Dadi and Dadu both take medicines, give each of them their own box or basket, clearly labelled. A weekly pill box per person, with morning, afternoon and night sections, makes it obvious whether a dose has been taken.

Keep children's medicines and everyone else's well away from the elders' boxes, and out of reach of small kids.

## Write it down where everyone can see

A simple chart on the fridge for each elder helps more than any family discussion:

- Name of each medicine and what it's for
- Dose and timing, like 1-0-1, before or after food
- A tick box for each dose, each day

Whoever gives the dose ticks it. Now nobody has to guess.

## Shared phones need clear profiles

In many homes, one smartphone is shared by two or three people, or an elder uses a basic phone. If you use a health app, make sure each person has their own profile so readings and reminders don't get mixed. Ideally, the caregiver can manage everyone's profile from their own phone.

And whatever system you use, if anyone notices warning signs like chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden weakness, confusion or fainting, don't wait to discuss it in the family. Call 112 or get urgent medical help.

Family profiles, so one phone can manage medicines for several people without mixing them up, are a core part of what we're building at AICA. The waitlist is at aicaglobal.in.

## The takeaway

In a joint family, love is never the problem. Coordination is. Name an anchor, separate the medicines, put a tick chart on the fridge, and give each person their own profile.
