You're standing at the chemist's counter, holding Papa's new prescription. Next to one medicine it says "1-0-1." Next to another, "BD × 5 days." At the bottom, something that looks like "SOS," which feels slightly alarming for a cough syrup. The chemist uncle reads it in two seconds. You've been staring at it for two minutes.

Prescription shorthand is a language of its own. Once you know the basics, it's much less mysterious.

## The number pattern: 1-0-1 and friends

In India, many doctors write doses as three numbers separated by dashes. Each number stands for a time of day: **morning - afternoon - night.**

- **1-0-1** means one tablet in the morning, none in the afternoon, one at night
- **1-1-1** means one tablet three times a day
- **0-0-1** means one tablet at night only
- **1-0-0** means one tablet in the morning only
- **½-0-½** means half a tablet in the morning and half at night

Some doctors use a four-number version that adds an evening dose. If you see four numbers, ask the pharmacist to confirm which slot is which.

## The Latin-ish abbreviations

These come from old medical Latin and are still used widely. Here are the common ones:

- **OD:** once a day
- **BD** or **BID:** twice a day
- **TDS** or **TID:** three times a day
- **QID:** four times a day
- **HS:** at bedtime
- **SOS:** only if needed, for example when there's pain or fever. Not every day
- **STAT:** immediately, right now
- **AC:** before food
- **PC:** after food

So "BD PC" means twice a day, after food. "1 tab HS" means one tablet at bedtime.

## Duration and quantity

Next to the dose, you'll often see how long to take it.

- **× 5 days** or **5/7** means for five days
- **× 2 weeks** or **2/52** means for two weeks
- **× 1 month** or **1/12** means for one month
- **Cont.** or **continue** usually means keep taking it until the doctor reviews it

That last one is important. "Continue" medicines, often for BP, sugar or thyroid, are meant to be taken regularly, not stopped when the strip finishes. If you're unsure whether something is a short course or a long-term medicine, ask.

## Words you might see

A few other terms that turn up:

- **Tab** for tablet, **Cap** for capsule, **Syp** for syrup
- **Inj** for injection
- **mg** and **ml** for strength and amount
- **Rx** at the top simply marks the start of the prescription

## When the handwriting wins

Here's the honest bit. Even if you know every abbreviation, doctor handwriting can still defeat you. A "1" can look like a "7." "OD" can look like "BD." Two medicine names can look nearly identical.

So a few simple rules:

- **Never guess.** If something is unclear, ask the pharmacist or call the clinic.
- **Ask the chemist to write it down plainly.** Most will happily write "subah 1, raat 1" on the strip or box.
- **Read it back.** Before you leave the counter, say out loud what you understood: "So this one morning and night, after food, for five days?"
- **Keep a clear photo** of the prescription so you can check it again at home.

If anything about a dose seems off, like it's much more than before, check with the doctor or pharmacist before giving it.

## The takeaway

1-0-1 is morning, afternoon, night. OD is once, BD twice, TDS thrice, SOS only when needed. When in doubt, don't decode alone: ask the pharmacist to confirm.
