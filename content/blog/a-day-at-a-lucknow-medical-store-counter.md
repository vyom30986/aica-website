The shutter goes up a little after 8 am. Somewhere down the lane, a pressure cooker whistles. Before the lights are even fully on, someone is already at the counter holding out a folded prescription and a strip with two tablets left. "Bhaiya, yeh wali de do. Same." This is a picture of what a typical day can look like at a neighbourhood medical store in Lucknow. Not one specific shop, but a mix of moments anyone who's stood at that counter will recognise.

## The morning rush

Mornings are for regulars. People on their way to work, picking up the BP or sugar medicine for a parent. Retired uncles on their morning walk who stop for a refill and a chat about the weather.

The chemist knows many of them by face, some by name, and quite a few by medicine. "Aapke Papa wali?" is a real question, and it's usually right.

There's also the first wave of paper prescriptions from nearby clinics. Some neatly printed. Some written fast, at the end of a long OPD, in handwriting only experience can read.

## Reading the handwriting

This is a skill nobody gets a certificate for. A pharmacist looks at a scribble and works out the medicine name, the strength and whether it's "1-0-1" or "1-1-1."

When it's genuinely unclear, a careful chemist doesn't guess. They might:

- Ask the customer which doctor wrote it and what it's for
- Compare with the patient's previous purchases
- Call the clinic to confirm
- Ask the customer to check back with the doctor

It takes a few extra minutes, and sometimes customers get impatient. But it's the right call.

## The afternoon lull

Afternoons are quieter. This is when stock gets checked, deliveries arrive and the shelves get sorted. It's also when phone calls come in.

"Aapke paas yeh hai?" "Kal tak aa jayegi?" "Ghar pe bhej doge?"

Some calls are from adult children in other cities, trying to sort out a parent's refill from far away. Sometimes they send a photo of the prescription on WhatsApp and ask for it to be kept ready.

## The evening crowd

After work, the counter fills up again. A mother with a child who has a fever. Someone who needs something for acidity after a heavy meal. A family member collecting a month's supply for a grandparent.

This is when the chemist ends up answering a lot of questions:

- "Isko khaane ke pehle lena hai ya baad mein?"
- "Yeh aur woh saath mein le sakte hain?"
- "Ek dose miss ho gaya, ab kya karein?"

A good pharmacist answers the general questions and, when it's beyond the counter, sends people back to their doctor. And when someone describes chest pain, sudden weakness, trouble breathing or fainting, the advice is simple: don't wait here, go to a hospital or call 112.

## The things nobody sees

What the customer sees is a few minutes at the counter. What they don't see is the memory work. Which customer is due for a refill. Which family usually buys which medicine. Who hasn't come in this month and might have run out.

Most of this lives in the chemist's head, a register or a notebook under the counter. It works, mostly. But it depends heavily on one person remembering everything, every day.

## Closing time

By 10 pm, the shutter comes down halfway. One last customer ducks under it. "Bas ek strip, please." It's sold, the register is closed and the lights go off.

Tomorrow, the same lane, the same regulars, a new pile of handwritten prescriptions.

## The takeaway

The neighbourhood chemist is part pharmacist, part translator, part memory bank for the families around them. Much of that work is invisible.
