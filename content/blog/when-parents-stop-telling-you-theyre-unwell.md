You find out from your aunt. Not from Mummy, not from Papa, but from Chachi, on a casual call about something else entirely. "Arre, tumhare Papa ka toh pichhle hafte chakkar aaya tha na? Doctor ke paas gaye the." You didn't know. Nobody told you. And when you call home, Papa says, "Kuch nahi tha, chhoti si baat thi."

It stings. Not because it was serious, but because you were left out.

## Why parents go quiet

It's rarely about trust. Most parents who stop sharing health news are trying to protect you, or themselves. A few familiar reasons:

- **"Bachche pareshaan ho jayenge."** They don't want you worrying in another city, especially if you can't do anything immediately.
- **They don't want to be a burden.** Asking for help feels like losing independence.
- **They fear the reaction.** If the last time they mentioned a symptom you panicked, lectured or booked five tests, they'll think twice next time.
- **They genuinely think it's minor.** A bit of breathlessness, a few bad nights, some swelling in the feet. "Age ka asar hai."
- **They're scared.** Saying it out loud makes it real.

None of this is unusual. But it means the information you need often reaches you late, second-hand, or not at all.

## Signs something is going unsaid

You can often notice changes even when nobody mentions them. Pay attention to small shifts:

- They sound tired more often, or end calls quickly
- They've stopped going for their usual walk or temple visit
- Medicine strips are running out slower than they should, or faster
- They mention a doctor visit vaguely, without details
- Other relatives seem to know more than you do

One of these on its own means little. A cluster is worth a gentle conversation.

## How to make it safe to tell you

The way you respond to small news decides whether you hear big news.

**Stay calm on the first telling.** When Papa mentions a dizzy spell, your first sentence matters. "Achha, kab hua? Ab kaisa lag raha hai?" works better than "WHAT? Why didn't you tell me earlier?"

**Thank them for telling you.** It sounds odd, but it helps. "Good that you told me" makes the next time easier.

**Don't jump to solutions instantly.** Listen first. Then ask what they'd like to do. Booking an appointment without asking can feel like being overruled.

**Share your own small stuff.** If you mention your own back pain or the cold you had, health talk becomes normal instead of an alarm.

**Ask about specific things.** "How's your sleep been?" or "What did the doctor say about the sugar?" gets more than "Sab theek?"

## Build a quiet safety net

You don't have to rely only on what your parents choose to tell you. Without going behind their back, you can set up a few things together:

- Ask if you can be added as a contact at their regular clinic, with their consent
- Get to know the local chemist they trust
- Keep a shared note of medicines and recent readings that you both can see
- Have one nearby relative or neighbour who can check in if needed

Talk about these openly. It's support, not spying.

## Know what can't wait

Some symptoms should never be kept for "baad mein bataenge." If a parent mentions chest pain, sudden weakness or numbness, confusion, slurred speech, trouble breathing or fainting, they need urgent medical help. Make sure they and whoever lives with them know to call 112 or go to the nearest hospital right away, and inform you after. Say this clearly, once, on a calm day, so it's not new information during an emergency.

## The takeaway

Parents go quiet out of love, not secrecy. Stay calm, ask specific questions and make it easy for them to tell you the small things. That's how you hear the important ones in time.
